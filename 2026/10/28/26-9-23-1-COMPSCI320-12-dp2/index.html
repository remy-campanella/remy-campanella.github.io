Revision Focus

Sequence alignment occurs in the 2022-2024 MCQs; 2024 SAQ5 requires both a grid DP value and its path. Practise table filling and reconstruction first. Understand Hirschberg’s time/space tradeoff without prioritizing its full implementation. Bellman-Ford is a second example of defining a state by a resource bound, here the allowed number of edges.

Sequence Alignment

Edit Distance and Alignment

For strings \(X=x_1\cdots x_m\) and \(Y=y_1\cdots y_n\), align characters without reusing them and without crossing pairings. Unpaired characters incur gap penalty \(\delta\geq0\). Pairing \(x_i\) with \(y_j\) incurs \(\alpha_{x_i y_j}\), usually zero for a match and a positive mismatch penalty otherwise.

The objective is minimum total penalty. Ordinary unit-cost edit distance is the special case of unit insertion/deletion and substitution costs; the course alignment model allows different penalties.

Problem Structure and Recurrence

Let \(D[i,j]\) be the minimum cost of aligning prefixes \(x_1\cdots x_i\) and \(y_1\cdots y_j\). \[ \begin{aligned} D[0,0]&=0,&D[i,0]&=i\delta,&D[0,j]&=j\delta,\\ D[i,j]&=\min\{D[i-1,j-1]+\alpha_{x_i y_j},\ D[i-1,j]+\delta,\ D[i,j-1]+\delta\}. \end{aligned} \]

Correctness. The final alignment column pairs both final characters, pairs \(x_i\) with a gap, or pairs \(y_j\) with a gap. Removing that column leaves an optimal alignment of the corresponding prefixes; otherwise replacing the prefix would improve the whole solution.

Fill rows or columns in increasing order. Answer: \(D[m,n]\). Time and full-table space: \(O(mn)\).

Implementation and Reconstruction

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Align}
\begin{algorithmic}
\PROCEDURE{Align}{$X,Y,\delta,\alpha$}
    \STATE Initialize $D[i,0]=i\delta$ and $D[0,j]=j\delta$
    \FOR{$i \gets 1$ \TO $m$}
        \FOR{$j \gets 1$ \TO $n$}
            \STATE $D[i,j] \gets \min\{D[i-1,j-1]+\alpha_{x_i y_j},D[i-1,j]+\delta,D[i,j-1]+\delta\}$
            \STATE Save a predecessor attaining this minimum
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $D[m,n]$ and the alignment recovered by tracing predecessors backward
\ENDPROCEDURE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Trace from \((m,n)\) to \((0,0)\), outputting paired characters or gaps, then reverse the output. Ties may yield multiple optimal alignments. If a mismatch costs more than two gaps, leaving both characters unpaired may be cheaper; do not force a diagonal step.

For the value alone, retain two rows, using \(O(\min\{m,n\})\) extra space after choosing the shorter dimension. This rolling table does not directly retain the path.

Hirschberg’s Algorithm

Sequence Alignment in Linear Space

The DP table is a shortest-path grid: horizontal/vertical edges cost \(\delta\), and diagonal edges cost the corresponding match/mismatch penalty.

To match the lecture’s indexing, split \(Y\) at column \(j=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\). Define

  • \(f(i,j)\): shortest grid-path cost from \((0,0)\) to \((i,j)\), equal to the prefix alignment cost.
  • \(g(i,j)\): shortest grid-path cost from \((i,j)\) to \((m,n)\), computed backward on suffixes.

Any path through \((i,j)\) has minimum possible cost \(f(i,j)+g(i,j)\). Choose \[ q\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{0\leq i\leq m}\{f(i,j)+g(i,j)\}. \] Some optimal alignment path crosses the middle column at \((q,j)\). Recursively solve the prefix and suffix rectangles and concatenate their alignments. Splitting \(X\) at a middle row instead is an equivalent transposed implementation; the two conventions must not be mixed in the indices.

Recursive Implementation

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Hirschberg}
\begin{algorithmic}
\PROCEDURE{Hirschberg}{$X,Y$}
    \STATE $m\gets|X|$; $n\gets|Y|$
    \IF{$m\leq2$ or $n\leq2$}
        \RETURN an optimal alignment from the ordinary DP, including empty-string cases
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $j\gets\lfloor n/2\rfloor$
    \STATE Compute $F[i]=f(i,j)$ for all $0\leq i\leq m$ using rolling columns
    \STATE Compute $B[i]=g(i,j)$ for all $0\leq i\leq m$ using rolling columns backward
    \STATE Choose $q$ minimizing $F[i]+B[i]$
    \STATE Release the temporary DP arrays
    \STATE $A_1\gets$ \CALL{Hirschberg}{$X[1..q],Y[1..j]$}
    \STATE $A_2\gets$ \CALL{Hirschberg}{$X[q+1..m],Y[j+1..n]$}
    \RETURN the concatenation of $A_1$ and $A_2$
\ENDPROCEDURE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Pass substrings by index ranges and append alignment output without repeatedly copying large strings.

Time and Space Analysis

A forward/backward pass costs \(O(mn)\) time and \(O(m)\) working space. Releasing the arrays before recursion leaves only constant-size split information per call. Including the output and recursive bookkeeping, the space is \(O(m+n)\).

For the lecture’s idealized even split, \[ T(m,n)\leq T(q,n/2)+T(m-q,n/2)+cmn. \] The bound \(2T(m,n/2)+O(mn)\) is valid but loose: it ignores that the two subproblems split the \(m\) dimension as well.

Proof of the tight bound. Choose \(c\) large enough to cover the direct base cases. Induct on \(m+n\), assuming \(T(m',n')\leq2cm'n'\) for smaller non-base rectangles. Then \[ \begin{aligned} T(m,n)&\leq2cq(n/2)+2c(m-q)(n/2)+cmn\\ &=2cmn. \end{aligned} \] Floors/ceilings only change the constant-factor accounting. Empty-string cases cost the length of the emitted gaps. For nonempty inputs, the total time remains \(O(mn)\).

Longest Common Subsequence

Prefix DP

A common subsequence preserves order in both strings but need not be contiguous. Let \(L[i,j]\) be the length of an LCS of \(X[1..i]\) and \(Y[1..j]\). \[ L[i,j]=\begin{cases} 0,&i=0\text{ or }j=0,\\ 1+L[i-1,j-1],&x_i=y_j,\\ \max\{L[i-1,j],L[i,j-1]\},&x_i\ne y_j. \end{cases} \]

If the final characters match, an optimal subsequence can end in that match. Otherwise a common subsequence cannot use both final characters as a pair, so omit at least one. Fill increasing \(i,j\); the answer is \(L[m,n]\). Time and full-table space are \(O(mn)\).

For recovery, move diagonally and emit a character at a match; at a mismatch, move to a neighboring state attaining the same optimum. Reverse the recovered characters. A rolling table gives the value in linear space; Hirschberg gives an actual LCS with linear space as well.

Reduction to Alignment

Set gap cost to one, match cost to zero, and mismatch cost to \(+\infty\) (forbid mismatching pairs). If the alignment pairs \(\ell\) matching characters, it leaves \((m-\ell)+(n-\ell)\) gaps. Therefore \[ \operatorname{LCS}(X,Y)=\frac{m+n-\operatorname{alignmentCost}(X,Y)}{2}. \]

The forbidden-mismatch condition is essential: ordinary unit substitution cost is not this reduction. This is another way to reuse the linear-space alignment algorithm.

Bellman-Ford-Moore Algorithm

Shortest Paths with Negative Weights

Negative edges invalidate Dijkstra’s settled-vertex argument. A negative cycle matters to an \(s\to t\) query only if it is reachable from \(s\) and can reach \(t\); then walks can have arbitrarily negative cost. A negative cycle elsewhere does not automatically destroy every shortest-path answer.

Dynamic Programming Toward a Fixed Destination

Let \(D_i(v)\) be the minimum cost of a \(v\to t\) walk using at most \(i\) edges: \[ D_0(t)=0,\qquad D_0(v)=+\infty\ (v\ne t), \] \[ D_i(v)=\min\left\{D_{i-1}(v),\min_{(v,w)\in E}\{l_{vw}+D_{i-1}(w)\}\right\}. \]

The two cases omit a new edge or choose the first edge \((v,w)\). If there is no relevant negative cycle, a shortest walk can be made simple, using at most \(|V|-1\) edges. Evaluate all vertices for each increasing edge budget. This destination-oriented convention must not be mixed with the source-oriented one below.

Course Implementation: Destination-Oriented Bellman-Ford-Moore

The lecture keeps \(d[v]\) as an estimate from \(v\) to \(t\) and stores \(successor[v]\), not a predecessor from \(s\). Initialize all distances to \(+\infty\) except \(d[t]=0\).

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Bellman-Ford-Moore}
\begin{algorithmic}
\PROCEDURE{Bellman-Ford-Moore}{$V,E,l,t$}
    \STATE Set $d[v]\gets\infty$ and $successor[v]\gets\mathrm{null}$ for every $v\in V$
    \STATE $d[t]\gets0$; $active\gets\{t\}$
    \FOR{$i\gets1$ \TO $|V|-1$}
        \STATE $next\gets\emptyset$
        \FORALL{$w\in active$}
            \FORALL{incoming edges $(v,w)\in E$}
                \IF{$d[v]>l_{vw}+d[w]$}
                    \STATE $d[v]\gets l_{vw}+d[w]$
                    \STATE $successor[v]\gets w$
                    \STATE $next\gets next\cup\{v\}$
                \ENDIF
            \ENDFOR
        \ENDFOR
        \IF{$next=\emptyset$}
            \RETURN $d$ and $successor$
        \ENDIF
        \STATE $active\gets next$
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $d$ and $successor$
\ENDPROCEDURE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The set \(active\) snapshots the vertices changed in the preceding pass. Incoming adjacency lists avoid scanning all edges separately for each active vertex. Updates are strict; arbitrary successor changes on equal values are unnecessary.

Correctness invariants, assuming no negative cycles.

  • Every finite \(d[v]\) is the cost of some \(v\to t\) walk; hence it never underestimates the optimum.
  • Distances only decrease.
  • After pass \(i\), \(d[v]\) is at most the cost of a shortest \(v\to t\) walk using at most \(i\) edges.

For the third property, take the first edge \((v,w)\) of such a walk. If \(d[w]\) improved in the previous pass, its incoming edges are reconsidered. If not, the required relaxation was already available earlier. Combined with a shortest simple path’s at-most-\(|V|-1\) edges, this proves correctness and \(O(|V||E|)\) time with \(O(|V|)\) extra space after adjacency-list preparation.

In-place versus synchronous DP. After pass \(i\), the in-place estimate need not equal \(D_i(v)\): an update can propagate through several edges within a pass. The invariant is the one-sided bound above.

Successor recovery. During execution, current successor pointers need not describe a walk of cost exactly the current \(d[v]\), because downstream labels can improve later. At termination with no relevant negative cycle, following successors from a reachable vertex gives a shortest route to \(t\). The lecture proves that a directed cycle in the strict-update successor graph would be negative; no-negative-cycle assumptions therefore exclude such a loop.

Standard Source-Oriented Implementation

Set \(d[s]=0\) and \(d[v]=+\infty\) otherwise. Perform \(|V|-1\) passes over the edges, relaxing \[ d[v]\gets\min\{d[v],d[u]+l_{uv}\} \] when \(d[u]\) is finite. Save predecessor \(u\) on a strict improvement. Stop early if a pass changes nothing.

After these passes, an additional successful relaxation reachable from \(s\) indicates a source-reachable negative cycle. To identify destinations with unbounded-below distance, propagate reachability from vertices still improved. For a single target, check whether these vertices can reach it.

Time is \(O(|V||E|)\) and space \(O(|V|)\) beyond the graph. Synchronous DP uses the previous layer exactly; an in-place pass may propagate through more than one edge, but retains the standard Bellman-Ford correctness guarantee.

Detecting a Negative Cycle Anywhere

A single source misses disconnected negative cycles; a single destination misses cycles unable to reach that destination. The lecture adds a new sink reachable by an added edge from every vertex. An equivalent source-oriented method adds a super-source with zero-cost edges to all vertices, or simply initializes every original distance to zero.

With \(n=|V|\) original vertices, relax every edge for \(n\) full passes. If a strict improvement occurs in pass \(n\), a negative cycle exists. If none occurs, the labels have converged and every edge satisfies \(d[v]\leq d[u]+l_{uv}\); summing around a cycle rules out a negative total.

Recovering a cycle. Store a predecessor on each strict improvement. From a vertex updated on pass \(n\), follow predecessors \(n\) times to enter a cycle, then continue until a vertex repeats. Reverse the predecessor traversal to list its directed edges. This uses \(O(n)\) additional space and \(O(n|E|)\) time. This paragraph uses the source-oriented predecessor convention; the lecture’s destination-oriented construction uses successors instead.

Currency-Conversion Application

If an edge exchanges one unit of currency \(u\) for \(r_{uv}>0\) units of currency \(v\), a cycle is profitable exactly when the product of its rates exceeds one. Assign \[ l_{uv}=-\ln r_{uv}. \] Then a product greater than one is equivalent to a negative sum, so negative-cycle detection finds the corresponding opportunity in the mathematical model. This is the course’s modelling example; practical fees and numerical tolerances would change the data and comparisons.

Distance-Vector Routing

A router can update an estimate to destination \(t\) using only neighbors’ advertised distances: \[ d(v,t)\gets\min_{(v,w)\in E}\{l_{vw}+d(w,t)\}. \] This is the local Bellman-Ford recurrence. Under a fixed network and suitable fair updates, routers need not run in perfect lockstep. A router stores distance estimates and next hops for the possible destinations.

Count to infinity. If a link fails, two routers may each believe the other still has a route, repeatedly increasing their estimates while routing in a loop. Thus convergence under a fixed graph does not mean arbitrary changes are instantly handled. Protocols retaining additional path or topology information can detect more routing structure, at a storage/communication cost. Path-vector information and full link-state topology are different forms of extra information, not interchangeable descriptions of a single algorithm.

Longest Increasing and Decreasing Subsequences

A subsequence need not be contiguous. Let \(L[j]\) be the length of the longest strictly increasing subsequence ending exactly at \(A_j\): \[ L[j]=1+\max\left(\{0\}\cup\{L[i]:i<j,\ A_i<A_j\}\right). \]

Fill increasing \(j\), return \(\max_j L[j]\), and save the maximizing predecessor for reconstruction. Time is \(O(n^2)\); space is \(O(n)\). For a strictly decreasing subsequence, replace \(A_i<A_j\) by \(A_i>A_j\). Repeated values do not extend a strict subsequence.

Example: \((3,4,8,2,3,4,7,9)\) has increasing subsequence \((2,3,4,7,9)\) of length 5. The original heading said decreasing, but this example and recurrence are increasing.

Counting Boolean Parenthesizations

States and Base Cases

For operands \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and intervening operators \(op_k\), let \(T[i,j]\) and \(F[i,j]\) count full parenthesizations of operands \(i,\ldots,j\) evaluating to true and false.

If \(a_i\) is true, \((T[i,i],F[i,i])=(1,0)\); if false, it is \((0,1)\).

Split at the Final Operator

For each \(i\leq k<j\), write \(t_L=T[i,k]\), \(f_L=F[i,k]\), \(t_R=T[k+1,j]\), and \(f_R=F[k+1,j]\). Add the following contributions:

\(op_k\) True contribution False contribution
AND \(t_Lt_R\) \(t_Lf_R+f_Lt_R+f_Lf_R\)
OR \(t_Lt_R+t_Lf_R+f_Lt_R\) \(f_Lf_R\)
XOR \(t_Lf_R+f_Lt_R\) \(t_Lt_R+f_Lf_R\)

Initialize each non-base state to zero and sum over every split. The left and right counts multiply because their choices are independent. Fill increasing interval length. Answer: \(T[1,n]\). There are \(O(n^2)\) states and \(O(n)\) splits per state: \(O(n^3)\) arithmetic operations and \(O(n^2)\) stored counts. Exact counts can be large, so this is an arithmetic-operation bound.

Exam Transfer: Minimum-Cost Grid Path (2024 SAQ5)

Moves are right, down, or diagonally down-right. Both endpoint cell costs are included. Let \(D[i,j]\) be the cheapest cost from \((1,1)\) to \((i,j)\). \[ D[1,1]=C[1,1],\qquad D[i,j]=C[i,j]+\min\{D[i-1,j],D[i,j-1],D[i-1,j-1]\}, \] with out-of-grid entries treated as \(+\infty\) and \((1,1)\) handled separately.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Minimum-Cost-Grid-Path}
\begin{algorithmic}
\PROCEDURE{Minimum-Cost-Grid-Path}{$C,m,n$}
    \STATE Initialize all $D[i,j]$ to $\infty$
    \STATE $D[1,1] \gets C[1,1]$
    \FOR{$i \gets 1$ \TO $m$}
        \FOR{$j \gets 1$ \TO $n$}
            \IF{$(i,j)\neq(1,1)$}
                \STATE Choose a valid predecessor $(u,v)$ among $(i-1,j),(i,j-1),(i-1,j-1)$ minimizing $D[u,v]$
                \STATE $D[i,j] \gets C[i,j]+D[u,v]$
                \STATE $pred[i,j] \gets (u,v)$
            \ENDIF
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Trace $pred$ from $(m,n)$ to $(1,1)$ and reverse the resulting path
    \RETURN $D[m,n]$ and the path
\ENDPROCEDURE
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Correctness. Every path ends through one of the valid predecessor cells. Its prefix must have minimum cost for that predecessor; otherwise replace it with a cheaper prefix.

Time is \(O(mn)\); table and predecessor space are \(O(mn)\); tracing the path takes \(O(m+n)\). A rolling-row value-only solution uses \(O(n)\) space, but does not alone answer the paper’s requirement to output the path.
